# How Color Affects Feeling, Perception, and Behavior — An Evidence Review

**Prepared:** 2026-11-18, for the Color Theory specialist's training expansion (Dustin's direction: the color work is "actually more about feeling than anything else").
**Method:** Research-specialist standards — primary sources and meta-analyses first, claims triangulated across independent sources, source quality and claim strength graded separately.

## How to read the grades

- **Strong** — replicated findings, meta-analytic support, or well-controlled experiments with converging results.
- **Moderate** — real findings with meaningful caveats: small or old samples, lab simulations, correlational designs, or effects that hold in some contexts and not others.
- **Weak-or-pop** — claims that circulate in design/marketing writing but rest on failed replications, untraceable statistics, single small studies, or evidence that points the other way.

## The single most important distinction in this literature

Most color–emotion research measures **associations** — what people *say* a color means or feels like — not **elicited feelings** — what a color actually *does* to someone's mood, physiology, or behavior. The associations literature is large, consistent, and cross-culturally robust. The elicitation literature is small, fragile, and littered with failed replications. Jonauskaite & Mohr's 2025 synthesis puts it plainly: "we do not know if we feel colours, but we know that colours convey emotions." A practitioner who keeps that sentence in view will rarely overclaim.

## 1. The framework: color-in-context, and what the replication reckoning left standing

**Color-in-context theory (Elliot & Maier, 2014, *Annual Review of Psychology* 65:95–120).** The field's organizing framework: a color's psychological effect is not a fixed property of the wavelength but of the *context* it appears in and the associations — learned (red ink on errors, red stop signals) and possibly innate — that the context activates. The same red signals danger on a test cover, attraction in a dating context, and dominance in a contest. **Grade: strong as a framework** — it is the consensus position and it correctly predicts why single-hue "effects" keep failing to generalize.

**Ecological valence theory (Palmer & Schloss, 2010, *PNAS* 107(19):8877–8882).** People like a color roughly to the degree they like the objects they associate with it: blue is liked nearly everywhere because clear sky and clean water are liked nearly everywhere; brown and olive-green are disliked because feces and rotten food are. EVT quantitatively accounts for preference patterns, including cross-cultural ones. **Grade: strong for explaining preference.** Its practical corollary: associations are *object-mediated*, so a palette borrows the feelings of whatever it resembles — a green that reads "living leaves" and a green that reads "mold" are different colors psychologically even at similar hue angles.

**The replication reckoning.** Much of the famous experimental color-priming literature was built in the 2000s on small samples and flexible analysis, and it has not survived contact with preregistration:

- Elliot's own 2015 review (*Frontiers in Psychology*) concedes that the classic "long-wavelength colors are physiologically arousing" hypothesis has produced **mostly null results** under rigorous control (hue held constant, experimenters blinded).
- Direct, adequately powered replications of flagship findings have repeatedly come back null (detailed under red and blue/red–creativity below).
- Meta-analyses that correct for publication bias shrink the surviving effects to small — sometimes to zero.

**Grade for the pop canon as a whole: weak-or-pop.** What survives the reckoning is (a) associations, (b) the dimensional effects of saturation and brightness, (c) context-bound, small behavioral nudges for red-as-warning, and (d) effects of *scenes and light*, which are different things from surface hue. Each is graded in its own section below.

## 2. The dimensions that do the heavy lifting: saturation and brightness

This is the best-supported finding in the entire field, and the one design folklore most underrates: **how saturated and how bright a color is moves feeling more reliably than which hue it is.**

**Valdez & Mehrabian (1994, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 123(4):394–409).** The foundational dimensional study — 250 color samples rated on the Pleasure–Arousal–Dominance scales. The regression weights:

- **Pleasure ≈ .69 × Brightness + .22 × Saturation**
- **Arousal ≈ −.31 × Brightness + .60 × Saturation**
- **Dominance ≈ −.76 × Brightness + .32 × Saturation**

Brightness and saturation effects were strong and consistent across hues; hue effects were smaller and less consistent. Most pleasant hues: blue, blue-green, green, red-purple, purple, purple-blue. Least pleasant: **yellow and green-yellow** — at the full saturation of Munsell chips (see §4.4 for why that does not contradict yellow's joyful associations). Men's and women's emotional reactions were highly similar. **Grade: strong** (for the dimensional claim; the specific hue rankings are chip-context-bound).

**Wilms & Oberfeld (2018, *Psychological Research* 82(5):896–914).** The modern, tightly controlled successor: 62 participants, 27 chromatic colors varying hue, saturation, and brightness *independently* on a calibrated LED display, with skin-conductance recording. Saturated and bright colors produced higher arousal *and* measurably stronger skin-conductance responses; valence (pleasantness) was highest for colors that were both saturated and bright. Arousal rose along blue → green → red at matched saturation/brightness. Blue's valence advantage appeared only at high saturation. **Grade: strong/moderate** — one lab, but rigorous stimulus control and a physiological channel agreeing with self-report.

**Light vs dark, saturated vs greyish (Jonauskaite & Mohr, 2025, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*).** A systematic review of 132 empirical studies, 42,266 participants, 64 countries, spanning 1895–2022: **light colors are associated with positive emotions and dark colors with negative ones, transcending color categories** — i.e., the lightness pattern holds regardless of hue. Saturated colors pair with positive, high-energy emotions; greyish/desaturated colors with negative, low-energy ones. **Grade: strong (for associations).**

**Children (Boyatzis & Varghese, 1994, *Journal of Genetic Psychology* 155(1):77–85).** Children respond positively to bright colors (pink, blue, red) and negatively to dark ones (brown, black, gray), with bright-color positivity strengthening with age. **Grade: moderate** (small, old, but consistent with the dimensional literature and with later child-design research).

**Working summary:** if a palette needs to feel more joyful, the highest-confidence lever is *raise value and add chroma in the accents*, not "change the hue." If it needs to feel calmer and more elevated, the lever is *lower chroma and controlled value*, not "make it blue."

## 3. Warm vs cool: four claims, four different verdicts

### 3.1 Arousal — "warm colors excite, cool colors calm"

The association is real and shared (people reliably *describe* reds/oranges as arousing and blues/greens as calm). The physiological claim underneath it — that long-wavelength light intrinsically raises arousal — has **mostly produced null results** under rigorous control (Elliot, 2015, *Frontiers in Psychology*, reviewing the literature; the effect appears to depend on saturation and brightness, which early studies confounded with hue). Where arousal differences do appear at matched saturation/brightness (Wilms & Oberfeld 2018), they are modest and ordered blue < green < red. **Grade: association strong; intrinsic physiological arousal weak-or-pop; small matched-stimulus effect moderate.**

### 3.2 Perceived temperature — the hue-heat hypothesis

Two claims hide inside this one:

- **Labeling:** people reliably *call* hues warm or cool. Hammond et al. (2024, *Scientific Reports* 14:26852), 40 participants × 63 colors: red rated warmest (+3.75), blue coolest (−1.87) on a −5…+5 scale — **and every color was rated warmer as its intensity and luminance increased** (r² .78–.98). Intensity is a warmth lever in its own right. **Grade: strong (as an association).**
- **Felt temperature:** that a red room makes you *feel* warmer, enough to matter for comfort or thermostat settings. Bennett & Rey (1972) found no effect. Battistel et al. (2024, *Scientific Reports*): 26 participants, red vs blue LED-lit chambers at 23–25 °C, p = 0.29 — null. Bavaresco et al. (2026, *Building and Environment* 292:114264), a multi-site study with 464 sessions: no significant support for light-color-temperature hue-heat. **Grade: weak-or-pop.** Design implication: warm palettes make a space *read* warm; do not promise they make anyone feel warmer.

### 3.3 Appetite — the fast-food legend points the wrong way

"Red and yellow stimulate appetite — that's why fast-food logos use them" is repeated everywhere and supported almost nowhere. There is **no direct experimental evidence** that red or yellow increase food intake; logo colors are better explained by visibility, category convention, and imitation. The one hue with actual intake evidence is red — in the **opposite** direction:

- Genschow, Reutner & Wänke (2012, *Appetite* 58(2):699–702): participants drank less from a red-labelled cup and ate less snack food from a red plate than from blue/white equivalents — interpreted as a learned stop/avoidance signal operating outside focused awareness.
- Reutner, Genschow & Wänke (2015, *Food Quality and Preference* 44:172–178): the red reduction is larger for *unhealthy* food — consistent with a warning-signal reading, not an appetite stimulant.

Reviews of the red-boosts/blue-suppresses appetite literature call it inconsistent overall. **Grade: fast-food claim weak-or-pop (unsupported, and the best evidence contradicts half of it); red-reduces-intake moderate-weak** (a few small lab studies, plausible mechanism, needs more replication).

### 3.4 Approach, dwell, and buying in colored environments

The retail-environment literature is old, lab-based, and simulated — but directionally consistent, and its mechanism finding is the valuable part:

- **Bellizzi, Crowley & Hasty (1983):** shoppers were physically *drawn toward* warm-color displays, yet rated red environments as negative, tense, and unpleasant — approach and liking dissociate.
- **Bellizzi & Hite (1992, *Psychology & Marketing* 9(5):347–363):** two experiments with simulated stores — blue environments produced more simulated purchases, fewer purchase postponements, and stronger browse/shop inclination than red. The authors' own conclusion: **affective perception (pleasantness) drove the outcomes, not arousal.**
- **Babin, Hardesty & Suter (2003, *Journal of Business Research* 56:541–551):** blue fashion-store interiors beat orange on store evaluations, patronage, and purchase intentions — **but soft lighting combined with orange nullified orange's negatives and produced the highest perceived price fairness at identical prices.** Color × light interaction; congruence beat hue.

**Grade: moderate.** The transferable lesson is not "paint it blue." It is: *people linger and buy where the environment feels pleasant, and pleasantness is a whole-scene property — color, light, and fit with the setting — not a hue.*

## 4. Hue by hue: what the evidence actually says

### 4.1 Red — the most studied, the most exaggerated

- **Attractiveness.** Lehmann, Elliot & Calin-Jageman (2018, *Evolutionary Psychology*), meta-analysis of 41 sources / 98 comparisons / 6,682 participants: red raised attractiveness ratings by d = 0.26 (men rating women; 0.19 without one outlier) and d = 0.13 (women rating men) — with detected publication bias, clearest in the women-rating-men set. The same authors' preregistered replications found d between −0.11 and 0.10 — essentially zero — and two of the three concluded the effect is "small, potentially even nonexistent." **Grade: weak-or-pop as popularly told ("wear red to be irresistible"); at most a very small, context-fragile effect.**
- **Achievement/performance.** Lehmann et al. (2020, *PLOS ONE*) meta-analysis: the red-impairs-performance effect is likely real but **substantially smaller than the 2007 originals**, with publication bias and high context-dependence. Four replication attempts of the semantic red effect on verbal reasoning (Collabra: Psychology, 2020; ~1,400 participants total) found nothing (effect sizes −0.23 to 0.19). **Grade: moderate-weak, small, context-bound.**
- **Sport.** Hill & Barton (2005, *Nature*) reported red-clad Olympic combat athletes winning more, especially in close bouts. A 2025 *Scientific Reports* meta-analysis across 16 tournaments and 6,500+ contestants (1996–2020) found red won **50.5%** — no significant effect; the pre-2005 close-bout advantage (56%) has vanished in the era of electronic scoring, and reanalyses attribute part of the original pattern to tournament structure. **Grade: weak-or-pop today.**
- **What survives for red:** it is a potent *learned signal* — errors, stop, sale, warning — and signals get attended to. Red also reduced snack intake (§3.3), consistent with avoidance signaling. Used deliberately as a signal (alerts, errors, genuine urgency), red is well-founded. Used as a magic performance/attraction lever, it is not. **Grade: moderate for attentional/signal use.**

### 4.2 Blue — beloved, trusted, and routinely overclaimed

- **Preference.** Blue is the most-liked hue across nearly every population studied — EVT explains this via sky/water associations (§1). **Grade: strong.**
- **"Blue creates trust / calm."** These are *associations* — real, measurable, and consistent (blue pairs with calm, security, competence in association studies and brand-personality research) — but there is no good evidence that a blue surface *induces* calm or trust behaviorally, and blue-heavy retail results (§3.4) are better explained by pleasantness/congruence than by a trust mechanism. **Grade: associations strong; induced-feeling claim weak-or-pop.**
- **Blue light is a different substance from blue paint.** Short-wavelength *light* (~460–480 nm, the melanopsin/ipRGC peak) is physiologically **alerting**: 460 nm light suppressed melatonin roughly twice as much as 555 nm at equal photon density (Lockley, Brainard & Czeisler, 2003, *JCEM*; Brainard action spectrum peaks 446–477 nm); 2 h of 460 nm light produced greater melatonin suppression *and* a greater alerting response than 550 nm (Cajochen et al., 2005, *JCEM* 90:1311–1316); even 40 lux of 6500 K light increased alertness and melatonin suppression vs warm lamps (Chellappa et al., 2011, *PLoS ONE*). **Grade: strong (for light).** Conflating the two — "blue calms" said of a screen-lit evening space — is the field's most common practitioner error.

### 4.3 Green — the restorative evidence is about *scenes*, not swatches

- **Associations:** green pairs with nature, freshness, and "go" — strong and cross-cultural.
- **Restorative environments:** the real evidence base concerns *vegetation and natural scenes*, dominantly green in content but never hue-isolated:
  - Ulrich (1984, *Science* 224(4647):420–421): 23 matched pairs of gallbladder-surgery patients; the tree-view group stayed 7.96 vs 8.70 days, needed fewer potent analgesics on days 2–5, and drew 1.13 vs 3.96 negative nurse-note comments. Small, retrospective; Ulrich himself cautioned the brick wall was unusually monotonous and the result should not be extended to all built views.
  - Twohig-Bennett & Jones (2018, *Environmental Research* 166:628–637): meta-analysis of 143 studies, >290 million people, 20 countries — greenspace exposure associated with lower salivary cortisol, heart rate (−2.57 bpm), diastolic BP (−1.97 mmHg), and lower incidence of type II diabetes, all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, plus better self-reported health. Authors flag heterogeneity and some poor-quality studies.
  - Jiang, Chang & Sullivan (2014, *Landscape and Urban Planning* 132; 2016, *Environment and Behavior*): dose-response — stress recovery rose with street-tree canopy up to roughly a quarter-to-a-third canopy cover, then levelled; physiology and self-report did not always move together.
  - Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989; Kaplan, 1995): nature restores depleted directed attention via "soft fascination." Berman, Jonides & Kaplan (2008, *Psychological Science*) found nature walks improved directed-attention performance where urban walks did not; later reviews (Ohly et al.; Stevenson, Schilhab & Bentsen, 2018) found support on some attention measures and not others, with low-to-moderate effect sizes. **Grade for ART's specific mechanism: moderate.**
- **Grade: moderate-to-strong for green *environments* (living vegetation, canopy, natural scenes); weak-or-pop for the claim that green *paint/pixels* restore anyone.** A flower park trades in the real thing; a website can only borrow the association and the imagery.

### 4.4 Yellow — joy, with footnotes

- **Joy association.** In Jonauskaite et al. (2020, *Psychological Science*; 4,598 participants, 30 nations), yellow pairs with joy — but the strength is **climate-dependent** (Jonauskaite et al., 2019, *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 66:101350; 55 countries, n = 6,625): the yellow–joy link strengthens further from the equator and in rainier countries (Finland 87.68% vs Egypt 5.66%); in the full model it is a latitude-and-rainfall effect. Oregon — rainy, northern — sits in the strong-association zone. **Grade: strong (for the association, in climates like ours).**
- **The apparent contradiction.** Valdez & Mehrabian rated yellow among the *least pleasant* hues — at full Munsell-chip saturation, presented as isolated chips. Association studies use color *terms and concepts*; chip studies use maximally saturated samples. Saturation and task explain the split: joyful yellow in practice is usually a mid-to-light value (sunshine, butter, bloom), not a full-chroma chip. **Grade: the two findings are compatible; the lesson is that value/chroma placement decides which yellow you get.**
- **Overstimulation edge.** Claims that bright yellow "strains the eye" or overstimulates (babies crying in yellow rooms, etc.) circulate without solid experimental support; there is a plausible basis in yellow's high luminance and chromatic salience, and full-chroma yellow does fatigue in large fields — but as an evidence claim: **weak-or-pop.** Treat large areas of full-chroma yellow as a craft risk (glare, cheapness read), not a documented psychological hazard.

### 4.5 Pink and Baker-Miller pink — the canonical cautionary tale

Schauss (1979) claimed a specific pink reduced strength and aggression; the claim decorated holding cells across North America. Gilliam & Unruh (1988) failed to reproduce the strength/heart-rate effects. **Genschow et al. (2015, *Psychology, Crime & Law* 21:482–489)** ran the definitive test: 59 inmates randomly assigned to pink vs white cells in a Swiss prison — aggression fell in *both* conditions, with **no color difference at any point**, and the authors warn pink may backfire where it reads as an attack on masculinity. **Grade: weak-or-pop — refuted as an effect; invaluable as a lesson in how one weak study becomes a design "fact."**

### 4.6 The red-detail / blue-creative claim

Mehta & Zhu (2009, *Science*): red improved detail-task performance, blue improved creative-task performance. Steele (2014, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 21:771–776) ran a direct replication with the original materials and n = 263 (vs the original 69): **no color-priming effect; the interaction explained under 1% of variance.** **Grade: weak-or-pop as a design rule.** Do not choose interface colors by it.

## 5. Cross-cultural and individual variation: where "universal" breaks

**Cross-cultural associations (Jonauskaite et al., 2020, *Psychological Science*).** 4,598 participants, 30 nations, 22 languages, Geneva Emotion Wheel method: national color–emotion patterns correlate **~0.88 on average** — far more shared than pop "color means opposite things in other cultures" writing suggests. Stable patterns: red is the only color strongly tied to *both* a positive (love) and a negative (anger) emotion; pink–love/joy/pleasure; yellow–joy; orange–amusement; black–sadness/fear; grey–sadness/disappointment; white–relief. **But** nationality still predicts residual variation, tracking linguistic and geographic proximity — and specific associations (yellow–joy, §4.4) can swing from 88% to 6% by country. **Grade: strong.** Practical read: the shared core is safe to design on for a general audience; specific symbolic meanings (mourning colors, luck, purity) remain culture-local and must be checked per audience.

**Gender.** Hurlbert & Ling (2007, *Current Biology* 17:R623–R625) found a real but **small** sex difference along the red–green cone-opponent axis (women weighting redder hues, men blue-green; both sexes preferring violet over yellow-green). The hunter-gatherer explanation offered for it is post-hoc speculation, and "girls innately love pink" is pop overreach — pink preference in girls is heavily culturally trained and historically recent. Valdez & Mehrabian found men's and women's *emotional reactions* to color highly similar. **Grade: small preference-axis difference moderate; pop versions weak-or-pop.** Design consequence: never segment palettes by gender on this evidence.

**Age.** Two solid facts at opposite ends of life. Children: bright and saturated win, dark loses (§2). Aging: the crystalline lens yellows progressively, filtering short wavelengths — older viewers literally receive a reduced blue signal, and blue-dependent distinctions (blue/green, blue/violet) get harder. This is established physiology, not preference research. **Grade: strong (physiology).** For an audience that includes older gardeners, it means: never carry meaning on blue distinctions alone, and keep value contrast generous.

**Personal association.** EVT (§1) implies preference and feeling track *individual* object-associations; two viewers can read the same green as "garden" and "hospital." No palette reads identically to everyone — one more reason feeling targets are set as *ranges and dominant reads*, verified with real viewers, not as guaranteed effects.

## 7. Practical synthesis: using feeling-evidence without overclaiming

**The levers, ranked by evidence confidence:**

1. **Value (lightness) and value contrast** — drives legibility, the light = positive / dark = negative association, perceived warmth (with intensity), and dominance/submissiveness reads. The strongest, safest lever.
2. **Saturation/chroma** — drives arousal and energy: saturated + bright maximizes both arousal and valence; low chroma reads calm, elevated, quiet. The feeling "volume knob."
3. **Temperature (warm/cool balance)** — drives associative warmth, approach reads, and scene mood; intensity shifts it too. An association lever, not a thermostat.
4. **Hue** — the most context-bound lever: carries specific learned associations (red = signal, green = living things, blue = sky/water trust) that flip with context. Use hue for *meaning and association*, dimensions for *feeling intensity*.

**Working method for evaluating a palette's emotional read:**

1. **Name the feeling target as a position, not a promise** — e.g., "unhurried, warm, quietly joyful" — and the feelings to avoid.
2. **Set the dimensions first:** choose the value key (light/mid/dark overall) and the chroma budget (where saturation is allowed to live — accents vs fields). This decides most of the emotional read before hue is discussed.
3. **Assign hues by association fit** (EVT): what objects does this palette resemble, and does the audience like those objects? Check the resemblance, not the hue name.
4. **Check the signal colors separately:** anything red-family will be read as signal/urgency by default; confirm that's intended wherever it appears.
5. **Check light vs surface:** on screens and in lit spaces, the *emitted/displayed light* has physiological effects (alerting blue-rich light) that surface-color associations don't predict. Judge evening/night contexts by the light rule, daytime print by the surface rule.
6. **Verify with real viewers in context** — a five-person "what does this feel like, what does it remind you of" check beats any amount of folklore, because associations are the actual mechanism.

**The honesty rule.** Effect sizes in this literature are **modest and context-dependent**; associations are strong, induced feelings are weak, and behavior effects are small nudges that context can erase (the red advantage literally vanished when scoring technology changed). The defensible claim is always: "this palette is *built to read as* X, using the levers the evidence supports" — never "this color *makes* people feel/do X." Sell fit, not magic.
